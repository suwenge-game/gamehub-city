Making this material stick

Strategy reading decays fast without a retrieval habit, so pair this page with a concrete retrieval loop: after your next solve, close the page and write the one rule from here that applied to your board. If nothing applied, that is a data point too — it means the page's scope did not match your board's phase, and scope-matching is itself a skill this site teaches.

The second habit is a weekly audit. Pick your worst solve of the week — not your best — and locate the row where the plan diverged from the board. The divergence row almost always matches one of three failure classes: an unmapped yellow, an unnamed direction press, or a premature commit. Each class has a page here; the audit's job is routing you to it.

Third: share your reasoning, not your results. The score grid is a weak signal and a strong temptation; the reasoning chain is the actual skill artifact. Players who can explain a four-row solve in four sentences are the ones who keep improving — explanation forces the model of the board to stay explicit, and explicit models are correctable models.

A note on our data

Wednesday method: double-letter audit

Midweek boards have the highest doubled-letter frequency in the archive's history — a statistical artifact of the answer bank's generation. The Wednesday ritual is a thirty-second audit after row two: list the letters you hold evidence for, and ask whether any has two unexplained sightings. If yes, the doubled-letter reading from the strategy lab applies; if no, carry on with standard narrowing.

The audit costs seconds and catches the specific error that most often turns a three-row solve into a six-row grind: reading two yellows of the same letter as one yellow. The reading failure is perceptual, not logical — which is why awareness alone is not enough and the weekly audit is.

Wednesday pairs with the repeated-letters deep dive. If today's letter-set hint says 'repeated letters: yes,' the audit is mandatory reading before you guess — the two-yellow reading error is the single most expensive perceptual slip this game offers.

One more angle on today's board: the difficulty profile, category, and anchors above are the same three signals our editorial desk uses to grade the puzzle before publication, so using them well means reading the board the way its author did. That symmetry is the quiet advantage of a site that writes its own puzzles and its own hints — the hints describe the generator's decisions, not a stranger's guesses. If today's level three made the answer feel inevitable, you read it right; if level one already gave it away, that is a grading note worth mailing to the editor.
