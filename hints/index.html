Hint archive

Every day, four nudges

Each archive page layers hints the same way our editors debug a hard solve: difficulty profile, category, letter set, positional anchors, then confusable words. Yesterday's answer is revealed on the following day's page, never on the day itself.

How to use the archive

Struggling solvers should stop after level two — the letter-set clue plus category is usually enough. Streak chasers can compare their opener performance against the positional anchors to decide whether today favors information-heavy first guesses. Teachers printing the archive for word-work stations can use level three as the worksheet cutoff.

Making this material stick

Strategy reading decays fast without a retrieval habit, so pair this page with a concrete retrieval loop: after your next solve, close the page and write the one rule from here that applied to your board. If nothing applied, that is a data point too — it means the page's scope did not match your board's phase, and scope-matching is itself a skill this site teaches.

The second habit is a weekly audit. Pick your worst solve of the week — not your best — and locate the row where the plan diverged from the board. The divergence row almost always matches one of three failure classes: an unmapped yellow, an unnamed direction press, or a premature commit. Each class has a page here; the audit's job is routing you to it.

Third: share your reasoning, not your results. The score grid is a weak signal and a strong temptation; the reasoning chain is the actual skill artifact. Players who can explain a four-row solve in four sentences are the ones who keep improving — explanation forces the model of the board to stay explicit, and explicit models are correctable models.

A note on our data

Every number cited on this site comes from the same answer bank the daily puzzle uses, regenerated on bank changes and dated on each page. Generic strategy sites quote Scrabble-era letter tables; ours are measured on the words this site actually serves. When advice conflicts between sources, the conflict is usually stale data on one side — check which site shows its numbers before deciding which to trust.

The archive as a training curriculum

Beyond daily nudges, the archive doubles as a structured curriculum with a measurable arc. Weeks one through three build pattern recognition: the level-one and level-two clues teach you the category families and the letter-set vocabulary that experienced solvers read fluently. Weeks four through eight build position sense: level-three anchors become guessable before you test them. From week nine, most readers find they need only the difficulty profile line to play well — and that line is the entire difficulty model of the site expressed in one sentence.

Use the archive with a notebook rather than a scoreboard. For each day, write which hint level you needed and whether the confusable list contained the word you actually guessed. The confusables column is a mirror: it shows the specific neighbor words your mental lexicon confuses, which is precisely the vocabulary-training signal a word list alone cannot give.

Why hints come in four levels, not five

The four-level structure mirrors the natural decision points of a solve: choose a family, choose a letter set, commit to anchors, and confront the confusables. A fifth level (the answer) exists on yesterday's page only. Keeping hints and answers on separate days is deliberate — it preserves the puzzle for the next reader, and it forces the recap to be written as a debrief rather than a spoiler. Debriefs teach; spoilers end the lesson.

The level structure also distributes the difficulty fairly across the week's solvers. Early in your streak you will read all four levels; later you will need two. The archive measures that drift for you: your hint-level usage is the cleanest single metric of improvement the game offers, cleaner than streaks (which mix luck) and cleaner than raw solve counts (which mix board sizes).
