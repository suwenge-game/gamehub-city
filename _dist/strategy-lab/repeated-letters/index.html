Repeated letters: the pattern your brain refuses to see

About 28% of answers contain a letter used twice, yet repeated-letter boards are systematically misread. Here is why the error happens and the reframe that fixes it.

The blind spot

When a guess returns two yellows of the same letter, most players read the board as "this letter is in the word" — true but incomplete. The full reading is "this letter is in the word at least twice, and twice is now the likeliest case." The human pattern-matcher resists that conclusion because doubled letters are rarer in everyday text than in the answer bank.

In our measured bank, roughly 28% of answers contain a repeat: doubled consonants in the middle, twin vowels, or a letter bookending the word. Because the share is high, the two-yellow board should flip your prior hard toward a repeat — but only if you notice the two yellows are the same letter.

Reading two yellows correctly

A single yellow says: this letter is present, not here. Two yellows of the same letter say: present at least twice, not in either tested slot. The candidate pool after a correct two-yellow read is drastically smaller than after the naive read, which is why doubled-letter days feel impossibly unfair to players who miss it and trivially easy to players who see it.

The practical checklist on any two-yellow board: first, confirm the letters are identical; second, strike both tested positions plus all slots those letters cannot occupy; third, look for words with a genuine double — LL, TT, SS, OO, EE — placed where remaining greens allow.

Doubles by family

Not all doubles are equal. Middle-position doubled consonants (the LL/TT/SS family) are the most common; doubled vowels come next; and the same letter opening and closing the word is rare but devastating when it appears, because it constrains both ends simultaneously. When the pool is narrow and your greens permit it, testing a middle double is usually the highest-value probe left.

There is also the quiet repeat: a letter used twice non-adjacently, like a C in slots 1 and 4. These evade the "look for doubles" instinct entirely. The defense is mechanical — after three rows, list the letters you have evidence for and ask whether any has two unexplained yellow sightings.

Training the reflex

The hint archive makes training direct: open a past day, read only the difficulty profile line, and predict whether the answer contained a repeat before revealing anything. Ten days of this drill installs the check. Players report the strangest sensation — the two-yellow board suddenly "looks different," which is exactly the perceptual learning goal.

A second drill uses the eliminator style: play a week where every second guess deliberately includes one plausible double. You will lose a little coverage and gain a reliable feel for how often the double pays for itself.

The meta-lesson

The repeated-letter blind spot is the clearest example of a general truth: feedback in this game is evidence, not decoration. Every tile speaks. The skill stack is not vocabulary depth — it is reading everything the board said, including the sentences your brain prefers to skip.

Worked board: reading a live example

+

Concrete boards beat abstract rules, so let us walk one shape end to end. Row one returns two yellows in slots 2 and 4 and nothing else. Under the pair plan, row two keeps no yellows in place, relocates both to their next common positions, and adds three untested top-eight letters. Suppose row two lands a green in slot 1 and a fresh yellow in slot 5.

+

The candidate pool is now tiny — typically single digits on the measured bank. The endgame rule from our narrowing guide applies: enumerate the live candidates, check the two-away condition, and either commit or design one probe that covers both branches. Most boards of this shape finish in four rows with disciplined play, five with cautious play, and only drift to six when a yellow is mis-mapped along the way.

+

Worked board: the empty first row

+

The fully gray opener is the most instructive board in the game, because every letter of the guess is now excluded and the exclusion is worth more than players credit. With a five-distinct eliminator opener, a clean miss removes a large share of the bank at once. The correct emotional response is not disappointment but satisfaction: the search space just collapsed, and the pair plan's second guess now operates on a much smaller field.

+

Play the second guess for coverage of what remains, not for exotic letters. The measured tables say the top-eight letters still dominate the survivor pool, so a miss on row one simply re-weights which of the familiar letters you test next. Players who reach for rare letters after an empty row are paying premium prices for information the common letters provide at a discount.

+

Reader questions, answered

+

Does this strategy survive if the answer bank changes? Yes — the structure of the advice is to measure, not memorize. The tables regenerate from the live bank, and the decision rules (pair planning, mapping yellows, the two-away commit) are bank-independent. What shifts is the specific opener list, which is why the articles link to computed data rather than naming sacred words.

+

Should I use a solver instead? Solvers are excellent teachers and poor crutches. Play your own rows first, then run the same board through a solver and compare where your information usage diverged. The difference is usually a yellow you under-read or an untested letter family. Using the solver as the comparison rather than the answer is what makes it training.

+

How many days until this becomes automatic? Most players report the pair-plan reflex solidifying within two weeks of daily annotated play — the archive exists precisely to give you those reps with feedback. The endgame discipline takes longer, because narrow-board shapes arrive only a few times a week. Be patient with that one; it is also the highest-value layer.

Appendix: how we compute coverage and elimination

Coverage is the share of answers that contain at least one letter of the guess — a proxy for "how often this guess tells me something." Elimination is its complement for pure-miss value: the share of answers ruled out when every tile comes back gray. Positional coverage, used in the opener tables, additionally requires the shared letter to sit in the same slot as in the guess, which is the stricter and more useful measure once greens matter.

Three consequences worth internalizing. One: coverage and elimination are two sides of the same coin, so "safe" letters and "risky" letters describe the same property from opposite moods. Two: a five-distinct-letter guess strictly dominates any word with a repeat as an opener, because a repeated letter buys no extra information on row one. Three: opener quality saturates — beyond the top handful, differences of a percent or two are noise, and habit matters more than the last percent.

Appendix: a solved week, annotated

Take any seven consecutive days from the hint archive. For each day, reconstruct the decision sequence: what the board said after row one, which family the feedback activated, whether the second guess followed the pair plan, and where the finish came from. Seven days is enough to see your own pattern — most players discover they have one signature error that recurs two or three times a week, usually a mapping error on yellows or a premature commit against the two-away rule.

The annotation habit matters more than the puzzles. Ten minutes with the archive, a written line per day, and a weekly look at the lines: that loop converts random play into training, and training is what a streak actually measures.

Appendix: when the advice runs out

Every guide on this site reduces uncertainty; none removes it. The bank contains words that behave unrepresentatively, boards that mislead, and days where the best plan loses to a well-read coin flip. When the advice runs out, the residual is variance, and variance is not a verdict on your play. The professional posture is the one poker players use: judge decisions by the information available when you made them, not by the tile colors that came back.

That stance is also the honest summary of this lab. Measure the bank, plan the pair, read every tile, commit at two-away, annotate the misses — and accept the days the word simply wins.
