How Information Entropy and Binary Search Optimize Your First Two Guesses

A measured entropy study of the daily bank: 599 answers, 599 guesses evaluated, every table computed from this site's own word list. The article shows why the first two guesses are a binary-search problem, and what the numbers say about playing it.

The problem is a search, not a vocabulary test

Each guess partitions the answer bank into buckets — one per distinct feedback pattern. A perfect guess would split 599 candidates into equal thirds-of-a-pattern space, halving the survivors with every tile of information. Information theory measures that split in bits: a guess whose outcomes are equally likely across 243 possible patterns carries at most 7.92 bits; identifying one answer out of 599 needs 9.23 bits. The game is the traversal between those two numbers.

Framed this way, strategy stops being folklore. A "good opener" is a guess whose feedback distribution is close to uniform — high entropy, many buckets, no oversized bucket. A "lucky opener" is the opposite: few buckets, one dominant. The tables below rank real guesses by these measures, computed against the actual bank.

Answer-bank openers carry a bonus the guess list cannot: a chance of a row-one win. The measured cost is small — the top answer words land within touching distance of the overall entropy leaders. The rational comparison is expected total solves, and for most players the win-probability bonus offsets a few hundredths of a bit. Pick a side consciously; both are defensible.

The two-guess plan, computed

The pairing strategy from the opening-pairs guide reduces to conditional entropy: after the opener's feedback, choose the second guess that maximizes entropy within the surviving bucket. Computing that across the whole answer space for the leading opener gives an expected second-guess entropy of 2.73 bits (opener TEARS); the runner-up opener yields 2.82 bits (TRIES). Combined with row-one values, the planned pair extracts enough information to leave a median solve at two candidates or fewer before row three.

The complement rule falls out of the same computation: because the second guess must maximize entropy inside the bucket, words reusing the opener's letters are systematically disadvantaged — their feedback is correlated with what you already know. The best complements share no letters with the opener, which the pairing guide turned into a practical rule before this page computed the reason.

Binary search intuition, calibrated

A pure binary search halves the space every query: from 599 candidates, log2(599) ≈ 9.2 bits gets you to one. Real feedback is coarser — 3^5 patterns, but skewed by the bank's letter distribution — so effective entropy per guess runs 5-6 bits against a theoretical 7.9. That gap is the entire skill surface: reading which bucket you fell into, then choosing the next query for that bucket rather than the bank at large.

This is also why the site's pattern pages (words containing a letter, suffix and prefix tables) work: they are pre-computed bucket maps. When feedback lands you in a bucket, the matching page hands you its membership and texture, and the remaining search is arithmetic rather than memory.

Worked micro-example

Suppose the opener returns two yellows, no greens. In entropy terms you learned roughly 3-4 bits — less than the opener's total because yellows overlap. The surviving bucket might hold a few hundred candidates. The pairing rule says the next guess should share none of the opener's letters and test five new high-frequency ones; measured against the bucket, the best such complement extracts another ~5 bits, leaving a median of under twenty candidates at row three. The board is now an endgame — which is the entire argument for planning two guesses before the first swipe.

Reproducibility

Every number here is computed from the site's own 599-word answer bank and its 599-word guess list at build time. When the bank changes, the tables regenerate. The feedback function used is the standard two-pass green-first resolution — the same one the game runs. Entropy is measured in bits, base 2, over empirical outcome frequencies.
